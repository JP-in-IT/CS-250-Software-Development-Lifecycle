# CS-250-Software-Development-Lifecycle
SNHU CS 250 final project: Sprint Review and Retrospective, Agile Presentation 

## How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How do user stories help?

For me, it starts with listening to what the user actually wants, not what I assume they want. User stories made that a lot easier because they force you to look at a feature from the user's point of view: who they are, what they need, and why. On the SNHU Travel project, writing stories with clear acceptance criteria gave the whole team the same target, so developers knew what to build and testers knew what "done" looked like. Breaking big requirements into small stories also made it easier to estimate work and catch misunderstandings early instead of at the end.

## How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I want to use in the future?

I like to work in small pieces, get feedback, and adjust. Coming from a security background, I'm used to plans changing on the fly, so Agile's flexibility made sense to me. When requirements shifted during the project, short sprints meant the team could adapt without throwing away work that was already done. Going forward, I want to keep using daily stand-ups to stay on the same page, sprint reviews to get real feedback from stakeholders, and retrospectives to actually fix what isn't working. I also want to keep writing tests alongside the code instead of saving testing for the end.

## What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member means communicating early, especially when something's blocked or behind schedule. It means understanding your role, whether that's Scrum Master, Product Owner, Developer, or Tester, and respecting what everyone else brings to the table. It also means being open to feedback in retrospectives instead of getting defensive. A team that's honest with each other ships better software than a group of talented people working in silos.
